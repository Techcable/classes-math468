I aim to prove the following statement: If $A$ is a matrix with positive real entries, there is a unique eigenvalue of largest magnitude, that eigenvalue is real, and the corresponding eigenvector can be chosen to have strictly positive components.
\section*{Lawler's Proof of Perron-Frobenius}
Define $\bu\ge \bv$ if $\forall i, u_i\ge v_i$ and likewise for the other comparison operators. 
\vspace{5mm} \\
Assume that $A$ is a $n \times n$ matrix with positive real coefficients. 
\vspace{2mm} \\
Lemma (a) claims that if $\bv\ge \bvz$ and $\bv \ne \bvz$ then $A\bv>0$. By def. of matrix multiplication: 
        \[ (A\bv)_i=\sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij}\bv_i \]
        Each term of the sum is nonnegative as $a_{ij}>0$ and $\bv\ge 0$. Since $\bv\ne 0$, at least one term $a_{ij}\bv_i$ is nonzero and so the sum $(A\bv)_i$ is positive. This is true for every $i \in \{1,\dots,n\}$, so in fact every entry of $\bv$ is positive and $\bv>0$. This proves problem (a), henceforth referred to as lemma (a).
\vspace{2mm} \\
If $\bv\ge 0$, define $g(\bv)$ as the largest $\lambda$ s.t. $A\bv\ge \lambda \bv$, so $g(\bv)=\max\{\lambda:A\bv \ge \lambda \bv\}$. Clearly $g(\bv) \ge 0$ as $A\bv \ge 0\bv =\bvz$ (still using $\bv\ge 0$). The function $g(\bv)$ is well-defined since $A\bv$ is bounded and the set $\{\lambda:A\bv \ge \lambda \bv\}\cap \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ is closed.
\vspace{2mm} \\
Lemma (b): Assume $\bv\ge 0$, I claim $g(\bv)>0$ and $\forall c>0, g(c\bv)=g(v)$. We already know $g(\bv)\ge 0$ from above. Using lemma (a), we know that $A\bv>0$. It follows that there must be some $\lambda>0$ s.t. $A\bv\ge \lambda\bv$ proving the first part of the lemma. Consider the equation $A(c\bv)\ge \lambda (c\bv)$. Dividing both sides by $c$ gives the equation $A\bv\ge \lambda\bv$ (this is valid since we are really just looking at $n$ equations in $\R$). It follows that the equations $A(c\bv)\ge \lambda (c\bv)$ and $A\bv\ge \lambda \bv$ have the same set of solutions $\lambda$ and so $g(\bv)=g(c\bv)$ for $c>0$.
\vspace{2mm} \\
% this is "Collatz–Wielandt formula"
Let $V=\{\bv:\bv \ge 0,\bv\ne \bvz\}, V_{\text{unit}}=\{\bv:\bv \ge 0, \bv \ne \bvz\}, \alpha=\sup g(V)$. \\
Lemma (c) says that $g(c\bv)=g(\bv)$ so we know that the value $\alpha$ is unaffected by the magnitude of the vectors being considered (assuming those vectors are nonnegative). In other words $\alpha=\sup g(V)=\sup(V_{\text{unit}})$. \\
The set $V_{\text{unit}}$ is closed and bounded, so it is compact. The function $g$ is continuous, as small changes in $\bv$ should lead to small changes in the maximum $\lambda$. It follows that $g$ attains a maximum on the set $V_{\text{unit}}$. As argued above, lemma (c) means that $\sup g(V)=\sup g(V_{\text{unit}})$. Since $g$ attains a maximum on $V_{\text{unit}}$ it attains that same maximum on $V$. This means $\alpha$ is not just the supremum of $g(V)$, but a maximum as well. As a result, there exists at least one vector $\bv_\alpha$ such that $g(\bv_\alpha)=\alpha$ and $\bv_\alpha \ge 0$. \\
\textit{NOTE}: While the value $\alpha$ is unique, there will always be multiple $\bv_\alpha$ due to the ability to rescale by a constant.
\vspace{2mm} \\
Lemma (c): I now claim that each $\bv_\alpha$ is an eigenvector of $A$ associated with eigenvalue $\alpha$. 
BWOC, assume that either some $\bv_\alpha$ is not an eigenvector of $A$ or its associated eigenvalue is not $\alpha$. In either case, $A\bv_\alpha \ne \alpha\bv_\alpha$. \\
Now consider the quantity $A[A\bv_\alpha-\alpha\bv_\alpha]$.
It follows from the hypothesis that $A\bv_\alpha - \alpha\bv_\alpha\ne \bvz$.
By choice of $\alpha$, $g(\bv_\alpha)=\alpha$, $\bv_\alpha\ge \bvz$ and $A\bv_\alpha \ge \alpha \bv_\alpha$.
It follows that $\mathbf{s}:=A\bv_\alpha -\alpha\bv_\alpha\ge \bvz$.
Since $\mathbf{s}\ne \bvz$ by hypothesis, it satisfies the requirements of lemma (a), so we have $A\mathbf{s}>\bvz$.
Using the linearity of $A$ we get $A\mathbf{s}=A^2\bv_\alpha-\alpha A\bv_a>\bvz$.
It follows that $A(A\bv_a)>\alpha(A\bv_\alpha)$. 
Since every entry of $A(A\bv_a)$ is strictly larger than every entry of $\alpha(A\bv_a)$, there exists some $\epsilon>0$ so that $A(A\bv_\alpha)\ge (\alpha+\epsilon)(A\bv_a)$.
However, by definition of $g$ and $\alpha$, $\alpha$ is the largest $\lambda$ satisfying $A\bu\ge \lambda\bu$ where $\bu \ge \bvz$ is an arbitrary vector.
Finding a larger value $\alpha+\epsilon$ satisfying the same inequality is a contradiction. 
\vspace{5mm} \\
Lemma (d): I now claim that the vector $\bv_\alpha\ge \bvz$ satisfying $g(\bv_\alpha)=\alpha$ is unique, provided that it satisfies the normalization condition $\sum_{i=1}^n (\bv_\alpha)_i=1$. \\
Define the operation $|\bv|_V=(|v_1|,|v_2|,\dots,|v_n|)$ to take the component-wise absolute value of a vector. 
BWOC, assume that the normalized vector $\bv_\alpha$ is not unique.
That is, there are nonnegative $\bv_\alpha'\ne \bv_\alpha$ both satisfying $g(\bv_\alpha')=g(\bv_\alpha)=\alpha$, and $\sum_{i=1}^n (\bv_\alpha)_i=1=\sum_{i=1}^n (\bv_\alpha')_i$.
Let $\bs=\bv_\alpha-\bv_\alpha'\ne \bvz$.
Then the normalization of $\bv_\alpha,\bv_\alpha'$ implies that $\sum_{i=1}^n\bs_i=0$.
Unfortunately $g(\bs)$ may not be defined, as some entries could be negative.
Instead consider $g(|\bs|_V)$. This is well-defined as $|\bs|_V\ge \bvz$ by definition. \\
% Actually not true:
% Because $|\dots|_V$ is defined componentwise and both $\bv_\alpha,\bv_\alpha'$ are nonnegative, we get $|\bs|_V=|\bv_\alpha|_V-|\bv_\alpha'|_V$.
Let $\beta=g(|\bs|_V)$.
Then by def. of $g$, $\beta$ is the maximum of the set $\{\lambda:A|\bs|_V\ge \lambda|\bs|_V\}$.
So we have $A|\bs|_V \ge \beta|\bs|_V$.
I claim that $|A\bs|_V\le A|\bs|_V$.
As $|\dots|_V$ is defined term-by-term, we can apply the triangle inequality to the def. of matrix multiplication to get $|(A\bs)_i|=|\sum_{j=1}^nA_{ij}s_i|\le \sum_{j=1}^nA_{ij}|s_i|=(A_{ij}|\bs|)_i$.
So this proves the desired fact $|A\bs|_V\le A|\bs|_V$.
As $\bv_\alpha',\bv_\alpha$ are both eigenvectors associated with eigenvalue $\alpha$ we know $A\bv_\alpha=\alpha\bv_\alpha$ and $A\bv_\alpha'=\alpha\bv_\alpha'$.
By linearity, $A\bs=A\bv_\alpha-A\bv_\alpha'=\alpha(\bv_\alpha-\bv_\alpha')=\alpha\bs$. Taking the component-wise absolute value of both sides gives $A|\bs|_V\ge|A\bs|_V=\alpha|\bs|_V$. Notice that $\beta$ is defined as the largest value $\lambda$ satisfying $A|\bs|_V\ge \lambda |\bs|_V$, so $\beta \ge \alpha$. However, $\alpha$ is definitionally the maximum value that $g$ attains for any nonnegative vector, so in fact $\beta=\alpha$. This means that $|\bs|_V$ is an eigenvector for $A$ associated with eigenvalue $\alpha$ and so $A|\bs|_V=\alpha|\bs|_V$. This means that the triangle inequality $A|\bs|_V\ge|A\bs|_V=\alpha|\bs|_V$ becomes an equality, giving $A|\bs|_V=A|\bs|_V$. The triangle inequality has equality only when all terms of the sums have the same sign, but this contradicts the fact that $\sum_{i=1}^ns_i=0$. 
\vspace{2mm} \\
Lemma (e): It also follows that $\bv_\alpha>\bvz$ because applying Lemma (a) to $\bv_\alpha\ge \bvz$ gives$A\bv_\alpha> 0$. Since $A\bv_\alpha=\alpha\bv_a$ and $\alpha> 0$ then $\bv_\alpha>\bvz$.
\vspace{2mm} \\
Lemma (f): I now claim that if $\lambda\ne \alpha$ is some other eigenvalue, then $|\lambda|<\alpha$.
The absolute value of $\lambda$ cannot be greater than $\alpha$ due to its def. as the supremum.
So we already have $|\lambda|\le \alpha$. \\
BWOC, assume that $|\lambda|=\alpha$.
Then there is some eigenvector $\bu$ associated with the eigenvalue $\lambda$, so $A\bu=\lambda\bu$.
Taking $|\dots|_A$ of both sides gives $|A\bu|=|\lambda||\bu|$.
Applying the Triangle Inequality gives $A|\bu|\ge |A\bu|=|\lambda||\bu|$.
It follows from def. of $g$ that $g(|\bu|)\ge \lambda$.
This means that $\lambda \in \R^+$ and so $|\lambda|=\alpha$. 
This contradicts the fact that $\lambda$ is distinct from $\alpha$.
\vspace{2mm} \\
Lemma (g): Let $B_k$ or $B$ be the $(n-1)\times(n-1)$ submatrix of $A$ obtained by deleting the $k$-th row and column (assuming $n>1$). 
I claim that all eigenvalues of $B$ have magnitude strictly less than $\alpha$. \\
\textit{Proof}: The arguments from lemmas (a)-(f) still apply to $B$.
We get some maximum eigenvalue $\gamma>0$ and a unique associated eigenvector $\bu_{\gamma} > \bvz$. \\
Let $\bu'$ be $\bu_\gamma$ with the addition of a zero to the deleted column.
I now claim that $A\bu'> \lambda\bu$ due to the effect of the deleted column/row.
Because $\bu$ is an eigenvector of $B$, we get $\gamma u_i=(A\bu_\gamma)_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}B_{ij}u_j$ for every row $i$. \\
Now we can translate this into the coordinates of $A$ simply by skipping over the deleted column/row $k$.
For each row $i \ne k$, we get $u'_i=(A\bu')_i=\big(\sum_{j=1}^k A_{ij}u'_j)$.
Changing from $\bu_\gamma$ to $\bu'$ translates from $B$ coordinates to $A$ coordinates and implicitly skips over the entry $A_{ik}$ because $u'_k=0$ by definition.
So we satisfy the eigenvector requirement for all rows except $k$. \\
In row $k$ we get $(A\bu')_k>u'_k=0$ since every entry of $A$ is positive and $\bu_\gamma>0$. \\
This means that $A\bu'\ge \gamma\bu'$, $A\bu'\ne \gamma\bu'$, and $g(\bu')\ge \gamma$.
Since $\alpha$ is the supremum of $g$ we get $\gamma \le \alpha$.
However, if $g(\bu')=\gamma=\alpha$, then part (c) would apply and $\bu'$ would be an eigenvector of $A$.
This is not possible because $A\bu' \ne \gamma\bu'$ (the inequality is strict in the deleted row).
Therefore, $\gamma \le g(\bu')<\alpha$ and so the maximum eigenvector $\gamma$ of $B_k$ is strictly less than $\alpha$. \\
\vspace{2mm} \\

Let $A_{\setminus ij}$ represent the matrix obtained by deleting the $i$-th row and $j$-th column of $A$ (so $A_{ij}$ still represents an element of $j$). \\
By def., the determinant is expanded in terms of the submatrices using the cofactor expansion along the first row.
